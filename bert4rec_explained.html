Bert4RecEmbedder, explained two ways

Same model, two audiences. Source: bvw/bert4rec.py (via bvw/embedder.py).

Where we're starting from

You know Word2Vec: an item gets a vector, trained so items that show up in similar local contexts end up with similar vectors. That's the model to keep in your head, because BERT4Rec is best understood as "what Word2Vec is missing."

  • Word2Vec looks at a small fixed window around each item and doesn't care about order within it.
  • BERT4Rec looks at the whole sequence at once and explicitly knows the order.

The mechanism that makes that possible is self-attention. That's the one new idea here — everything else (embeddings, a linear head, cross-entropy loss) you already know.

Attention, as an analogy first

Analogy Picture every item in the sequence as a person in a small meeting, each holding their own embedding as a "position paper." Before writing their final opinion, every person turns to every other person in the room (not just their neighbors) and asks: "how relevant are you to what I'm trying to figure out?" People who seem relevant get listened to more; irrelevant people get mostly ignored. Everyone updates their opinion based on this weighted listening, simultaneously. That updated opinion is the new representation for that position.

That's self-attention. Formally, each position produces three vectors from its embedding:

  • Query — "what am I looking for?"
  • Key — "what do I have to offer, as a label?"
  • Value — "what do I have to offer, as content?"

Position i's query is compared (dot product) against every position's key to get relevance scores, those scores are softmax-normalized into weights, and position i's new representation is the weighted sum of everyone's values. Do this for all positions at once and you've replaced "average over a fixed window" with "learned, content-dependent weighted average over the entire sequence."

Multi-head just means doing this several times in parallel with different, smaller query/key/value projections (n_heads = 4 here), so different heads can specialize in different kinds of relevance, then their outputs are concatenated.

Why "bidirectional" matters here

Nothing about attention forces position i to only look backward. In BERT-style models there's no causal mask, so every position can attend to positions before and after it. That's the "bidirectional" in BERT. A GPT-style model masks out future positions so it can only predict what comes next; BERT4Rec doesn't need that restriction because its training task isn't "predict the next item" — it's a fill-in-the-blank task.

The training task: Cloze / masked-item prediction

Instead of predicting the next item in the sequence, the model plays a fill-in-the-blank game:

  1. Randomly hide ~15% of the items in each sequence, replacing them with a special MASK token (Bert4RecConfig.mask_prob = 0.15).
  2. Run the whole (partially masked) sequence through the encoder.
  3. At each masked position, ask the model to predict which item was actually there, using a plain linear layer + softmax over the vocabulary.
  4. Backpropagate only through the masked positions (everywhere else is ignored in the loss).

Why is this a good training signal? Because to correctly guess the hidden item, the model is forced to use the surrounding context on both sides — which is exactly the representation- learning behavior we want for building a user embedding later.

Positional embeddings: how order gets in

Self-attention on its own is order-blind — swap two positions and the set of query/key/value interactions is identical. So before anything else, each item's embedding is added to a learned vector for "I am at position 3" (or position 7, etc). Now the same item behaves differently depending on where it sits in the sequence, which is the model's only way of knowing about order at all.

Contrast with Word2Vec Word2Vec has no notion of absolute position — "bought bread then milk" and "bought milk then bread" look identical to it. BERT4Rec can, in principle, tell these apart.

Putting the pieces together

One encoder layer, stacked twice here, does (in order):

1. Self-attention
Every position gathers a weighted mix of every other (non-padding) position's value vectors, as above.
2. Add & norm
The attention output is added back to its input (a residual connection, so the model can "skip" the attention step if it's not useful) and normalized.
3. Feedforward
A small 2-layer MLP (dim → 4×dim → dim) applied independently at each position, giving the model extra non-linear capacity beyond attention.
4. Add & norm
Same residual + normalize pattern again.

Stack that block n_layers = 2 times and you get progressively richer, context-aware representations per item.

From hidden states to one user vector

After training, the linear "predict the masked item" head is thrown away — it only existed to create a training signal. What we actually want is the sequence of hidden states themselves (one dim-64 vector per position), and embed() collapses that into a single fixed-size vector by averaging the hidden states across all non-padding positions. That's the "user embedding," directly comparable to a Word2Vec-based one.

The one-sentence version

Word2Vec gives every item a vector from "who tends to appear near me." BERT4Rec gives every position in a sequence a vector from "who in this whole sequence, in what order, is relevant to me," learned by playing fill-in-the-blank — and then we average those position vectors into one user embedding.

What it is

A compact BERT4Rec (Sun et al., 2019): a bidirectional transformer encoder trained with a Cloze/masked-item objective over interaction sequences, used here strictly as a user-embedding generator rather than a full recommender with a ranking head.

Architecture bert4rec.py:71-98

  • Token embedding + learned positional embedding, summed — order is encoded explicitly, not discarded.
  • nn.TransformerEncoder, 2 layers, 4 heads, dim 64, full bidirectional self-attention (no causal mask), padding excluded via src_key_padding_mask.
  • Linear head projects back to vocab size for the pretext task only; discarded at inference.

Training objective

Cloze-style masking: each non-pad position independently masked with probability 0.15, model predicts the original id at masked positions only (CrossEntropyLoss(ignore_index=-100)). Denser signal per sequence than next-item prediction (multiple masked positions per step), and lets the encoder use both left and right context to disambiguate.

Embedding production

Mean-pool final-layer hidden states over non-padding positions. Satisfies the shared Embedder protocol (embedder.py) — same fit(sequences) / embed(tokens) → dim vector contract as the Word2Vec baseline, for an apples-to-apples comparison.

Justification

vs. Word2Vec
Skip-gram is order-invariant within a fixed local window — captures local co-occurrence, nothing about sequence order or long-range dependency. BERT4Rec adds positional embeddings and full self-attention, so it can exploit ordering and arbitrary-distance relationships. That's the hypothesis this comparison is testing, not an assumed win.
Bidirectional cloze vs. autoregressive
Richer training signal per step (many masked positions vs. one next-item target), and avoids the exposure-bias asymmetry of always predicting the last item only.
Why so small 2 layers, dim 64, 8 epochs is a deliberate simplification, stated in the module docstring — the goal is a few-second CPU comparison, not a paper-scale reproduction. A faithful BERT4Rec would use more layers/heads, longer sequences, and far more data/compute.
Why mean pooling Simplest permutation-agnostic way to collapse a variable-length sequence into a fixed vector, and it mirrors how the Word2Vec baseline aggregates its own item vectors — keeping the comparison fair.

Limitations I'd flag unprompted

  • Small model capacity likely underfits richer or larger catalogs.
  • Mean pooling discards some of the positional/attention structure the model spent capacity learning — an attention-pooled or CLS-token readout might use it better.
  • 8 fixed epochs, no validation-based early stopping — tuned for a quick demo, not production robustness.

Attention as a matrix operation

Stack the sequence's embeddings as rows of a matrix X ∈ ℝⁿˣᵈ (n positions, d dims). Three learned linear maps produce

Q = X WQ, K = X WK, V = X WV,  with WQ, WK, WV ∈ ℝᵈˣᵈᵏ.

Then

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q Kᵀ / √dk ) V

Q Kᵀ ∈ ℝⁿˣⁿ is a matrix of pairwise dot-product similarities between every pair of positions. softmax is applied row-wise, so row i becomes a probability distribution over the n positions — the attention weights for position i. Multiplying by V turns each row into a convex combination of value vectors. So the whole operation is: build an n×n row-stochastic matrix from content similarity, then use it to take a weighted average of V's rows.

Why divide by √dk

If the entries of q and k are roughly i.i.d. with mean 0, variance 1, then q·k = Σ qjkj is a sum of dk roughly-independent terms, so its variance grows linearly with dk and its standard deviation with √dk. Left unscaled, dot products get large in magnitude as dk grows, pushing softmax into a near-one-hot regime with tiny gradients. Dividing by √dk keeps the pre-softmax logits at roughly unit variance regardless of dimension.

Multi-head as a subspace decomposition

Instead of one d → d attention, split into h heads (h = 4 here), each projecting into a d/h-dim subspace with its own WQ, WK, WV, running the identical softmax-attention operation independently in each subspace, then concatenating the h outputs back to dimension d. Each head can learn a different similarity structure (a different "which directions in embedding space count as similar" metric) rather than being forced to share one.

Positional embeddings break a symmetry

Self-attention as defined above is permutation-equivariant: if P is any permutation matrix, feeding PX instead of X produces exactly P applied to the output — the model literally cannot distinguish orderings of the same set of items. A learned positional vector pi added to each row (xi + pi) breaks that symmetry: it makes the input depend on absolute index i, not just on the multiset of embeddings, which is the only way position can enter the computation at all.

Residual connections and LayerNorm

Each sublayer computes x + f(x) rather than just f(x). This makes the identity map a trivial fixed point of training — if f isn't useful yet, gradients still flow through the x term unimpeded, rather than every layer needing to learn a faithful pass-through from scratch. LayerNorm then re-standardizes each row to zero mean, unit variance (with learned scale/shift), which keeps activation statistics stable as they pass through n_layers = 2 stacked blocks.

The training objective as MLE

At each masked position, the linear head + softmax defines a categorical distribution over the vocabulary, pθ(y | context). Training minimizes cross-entropy at masked positions only, which is exactly maximizing the log-likelihood of the true token under the model:

L(θ) = − Σi ∈ masked log pθ( yi | x\i )

where x\i denotes the full sequence with position i replaced by MASK. It's ordinary multi-class MLE, just evaluated on a random subset of positions per example rather than a single fixed target (as in next-token prediction).

Mean pooling as a linear readout

Let H ∈ ℝⁿˣᵈ be the final layer's hidden states and m ∈ {0,1}ⁿ the non-padding mask. The user embedding is (mᵀH) / (mᵀ𝟙) — the unweighted average over valid rows. It's the simplest linear map ℝⁿˣᵈ → ℝᵈ that's invariant to permuting the (non-pad) rows, which matches the fact that nothing downstream should care about where in the original sequence a signal came from, only that it's present somewhere.
