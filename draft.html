Why Swiss Tournaments Can’t Always Pick a Winner — and the Math That Explains It

Two titles, no clear winner

In December 2021, the World Rapid Chess Championship in Warsaw ended with four players sharing the top score: Nodirbek Abdusattorov, Ian Nepomniachtchi, Magnus Carlsen, and Fabiano Caruana all finished at 9.5 points out of 13. The title went to Abdusattorov — not because he was declared the best player on the day, but because he ranked highest on a tiebreak called Buchholz Cut 1, which counts the scores of your opponents minus your weakest opponent’s score. Carlsen was furious. He called the rules “completely idiotic,” arguing that either all four players should contest a playoff or none should.

Three years later, at the 2024 World Blitz Championship in New York, Carlsen and Nepomniachtchi finished a head-to-head tiebreak series at 3.5–3.5. Rather than continue, both players agreed to share the gold medal. FIDE’s president approved it on the spot.

Two world championships, two titles without a clear winner. Were these flukes — unfortunate coincidences at the top of elite fields? Or is something structural going on?

It’s structural. And the math makes it predictable.

Swiss pairing as a sorting algorithm

Swiss is the dominant tournament format when you can’t run a full round-robin. After each round, players are sorted by their current score and paired against similarly-ranked opponents they haven’t faced yet. The format is widely used in chess, Magic: The Gathering, esports, and academic hackathons. Its appeal is efficiency: you get a meaningful ranking from O(log n) rounds instead of O(n).

From an algorithms perspective, Swiss is adaptive comparison sort under noise. Each game is a noisy comparison between two players — the better player wins most of the time, but not always. The question is: how many noisy comparisons do you need before the ranking at the top is trustworthy?

To answer that, I ran a simulation.

The model

Each player is assigned a true skill drawn from a Gaussian distribution. In each game, both players draw a performance sample — their skill plus some random noise — and the higher performance wins (with a draw band in the middle to model the high draw rates in chess).

The key parameter is the variance ratio r, defined as the ratio of skill spread to per-game noise:

r = σ_skill / σ_noise

When r is large, players are clearly distinguishable — a strong player almost always beats a weak one. When r is small, the field is tightly bunched and games are mostly noise. Think of it as the signal-to-noise ratio of the tournament.

I swept across 7 field sizes (16 to 1024 players) and 5 variance ratios (0.25 to 4.0), running 200 independent simulated tournaments per configuration. For each tournament, I tracked two things: when the top-3 players by true skill first appeared in the tournament top-3, and what fraction of the true top-3 was correctly identified after any given number of rounds.

Finding 1: field size barely matters

The first result surprises most people. Doubling the number of players adds roughly 2 rounds to the expected convergence time. Going from 64 to 1024 players — a 16× increase — adds only about 8 rounds on average.

Field size Top-3 convergence rate Mean rounds to identify top-3
16 76% 22.1
64 53% 30.2
256 46% 34.4
1024 42% 37.0

The effect is real, and it’s logarithmic — consistent with the bracket-like structure of Swiss pairing. But it’s modest. If you’ve been thinking that bigger tournaments are fundamentally harder to resolve, the data says otherwise.

Finding 2: skill spread is everything

What actually drives round counts is the variance ratio. When players are tightly bunched in skill, the tournament struggles badly:

Variance ratio Top-3 convergence rate Mean rounds to identify top-3
0.25 10% 47.7
0.50 34% 41.4
1.00 62% 31.1
2.00 80% 20.3
4.00 86% 15.4

At r = 0.25, only 10% of simulated tournaments correctly identified the true top-3 within 50 rounds. At r = 4.0, 86% did. The field size changed little; the variance ratio changed everything.

Fitting the data to a formula gives:

rounds ≈ 2.20 · log₂(n) + 8.29 / r + 2.93

with R² = 0.85. The 1/r term dominates across the tested range. This is the core result: the question isn’t how many players you have — it’s how distinguishable they are.

Finding 3: quality improves with rounds, but with diminishing returns

The convergence framing above asks a binary question: did the tournament fully identify the correct top-3? A softer and often more useful question is: how good is the ranking after N rounds?

To measure this, I tracked overlap — how many of the true top-3 players appear in the tournament’s top-3 at each round — across a sweep of fixed round counts. At variance ratio = 1.0:

Players Mean overlap at 5 rounds Mean overlap at 30 rounds
32 1.59 / 3 (53%) 2.30 / 3 (77%)
128 1.10 / 3 (36%) 2.06 / 3 (68%)
512 0.79 / 3 (26%) 1.91 / 3 (64%)

Quality improves steadily but flattens — a quadratic curve fits much better than a straight line, confirming diminishing returns. Each additional round helps, but less and less.

The important implication: when a tournament ends with tied scores at the top, it doesn’t mean the format failed. It may simply mean the tournament stopped before the ranking had enough information to separate players who are genuinely close in strength. At that point, any tiebreak system is resolving genuine statistical uncertainty — not noise around a clear truth. There may not be a “right” answer.

Connecting to reality: estimating r from Elo ratings

So far this is all simulation. To connect it to real chess, we need to estimate r for an actual tournament.

The simulation’s win-probability model is Gaussian. Elo uses a logistic curve. These are close enough that we can match them, and the result is that the per-game noise implied by the standard Elo scale is approximately:

σ_noise ≈ 223 Elo points

This is the effective standard deviation of a single game’s outcome, in Elo units. Then r is simply:

r = (standard deviation of field Elo ratings) / 223

Computable from any published player list.

The payoff: Warsaw 2021 plugged in

The 2021 World Rapid Chess Championship had approximately 150 players and ran 13 rounds. The standard deviation of the field’s Elo ratings was approximately 146 points.

r ≈ 146 / 223 ≈ 0.65

This places the tournament between the r = 0.5 and r = 1.0 rows in the simulation tables. At that ratio, the model predicts:

  • Top-3 convergence requires ~30–40 rounds on average
  • Top-1 convergence requires ~18–23 rounds on average

They played 13.

And there’s a further complication: rapid chess has higher per-game variance than classical chess, meaning the Elo scale understates the actual noise in a given game. The effective r for rapid is probably closer to 0.5 than 0.65. That pushes the required round count even higher.

The four-way tie wasn’t bad luck. The format was running at roughly one-third of the round count the math says is needed to reliably separate the top-3 players in a field of that composition. Any outcome — including the one that happened — was essentially a draw from a highly uncertain distribution.

Worth noting: the organizers had reduced the round count from 15 to 13 that year, a decision that drew criticism in hindsight. Even 15 rounds would have been far short of what the model recommends.

The tiebreak debate reframed

Carlsen called Buchholz Cut 1 “completely idiotic.” The defense from organizers was that some tiebreak is necessary — you can’t just hand out four gold medals. Both positions are reasonable, and both miss the point.

Buchholz, Sonneborn-Berger, head-to-head record, coin flip — these are all methods for imposing a ranking on players the tournament hasn’t actually separated yet. None of them are “correct” in any deep sense when the underlying tournament hasn’t converged. They’re all arbitrary in the same way.

What can be changed is the number of rounds. More rounds produce a more reliable ranking, with predictable diminishing returns. If you want the top-3 to be reliable for a world championship with an elite field, the simulation suggests you need something in the range of 25–40 rounds, not 13.

In that light, the 2024 shared title — Carlsen and Nepo agreeing to split after an exhausting additional series — is arguably the most statistically honest resolution of the two. It acknowledged that the games played weren’t sufficient to separate two players who are genuinely close in strength.

Takeaway

Swiss tournaments are good algorithms. They’re efficient, adaptive, and fair in the sense that performance is what determines pairings and standings. But no algorithm can extract more signal than the data contains.

When elite players compete in a tightly bunched field, the signal-to-noise ratio is inherently low. The tournament needs many more rounds than most formats allow to reliably identify the best players. This isn’t a fixable formatting choice — it’s a consequence of the math.

For organizers: if you’re running a world championship with elite players and you want a clean result, the number of rounds matters far more than which tiebreak system you use. The simulation gives a concrete formula for estimating how many rounds you actually need.

For everyone else: the next time a major Swiss tournament ends in controversy at the top, consider that the problem may not be the tiebreak rules, the players, or the organizers. It may simply be that the tournament told us everything it could — and the players at the top were genuinely that close.
